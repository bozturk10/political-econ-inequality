# Regional context and case choice for the inequality presentation

## What the regional chart says

ECLAC reports annual percentage changes in household-survey Gini for two approximate periods. Its Latin American simple average fell by 1.5% per year in the early period and 0.7% per year in the later period. Brazil fell by 1.0% and 0.7%; Mexico rose by 0.2% and then fell by 0.4%. Peru fell by 2.2% and 1.7%. These are descriptive changes in national inequality, not effects of conditional cash transfers.

The period labels are approximate: ECLAC uses the available survey years for each country. It omits a comparable 2008–2014 change for Costa Rica because of a survey income-measurement break. The chart excludes Costa Rica for that reason. ECLAC data and the project World Bank WDI series are different data products and should not be spliced into one series.

Source: ECLAC, *Social Panorama of Latin America 2017*, Table I.1 and notes: https://repositorio.cepal.org/bitstream/handle/11362/42717/6/S1800001_en.pdf

## Case choice

**Recommended: keep Brazil and Mexico as the two explanatory cases.** Use the regional chart as the opening context. They are directly compared in published CCT research, and the project's existing Gini, bottom-quintile, coverage, and program-design evidence already covers both. The sharper pattern is Mexico's weak early decline in a region where inequality was generally falling, compared with Brazil's sustained decline. This is a descriptive puzzle; it does not establish that either CCT caused the national trend.

Working question: **Why did household income inequality decline more consistently in Brazil than in Mexico during the 2000s, despite conditional cash transfer programmes in both countries?**

Define the quantitative window before presenting results. ECLAC's broad context uses approximately 2002–2008 and 2008–2014; the existing WDI Brazil–Mexico analysis uses common observations in 2004–2014. Present the two sources in separate figures and explain their different survey series.

**Alternative: Mexico and Peru.** Peru's Gini decline was much faster in ECLAC's series, and its Juntos CCT began in 2005. This yields a sharper outcome contrast, but changes the research base and introduces more differences in growth, labor markets, geography, program age, and implementation. Do not switch solely because the Gini contrast is larger.

**Not recommended for the main question: all Latin American countries.** A region-wide chart is useful to establish the pattern and motivate case selection. Explaining CCT effects across 17–18 countries would require comparable program exposure, transfer generosity, labor-market data, survey definitions, and a much larger literature review.

## Competing explanations to assess

1. **Transfers and welfare structure:** CCT targeting, coverage, benefit size, and interaction with other social protection explain differences in immediate redistribution.
2. **Labor income and political economy:** wage compression, formalization, minimum-wage policy, and political coalitions explain more of the national Gini trajectory than the CCT transfer itself.

The direct transfer channel affects household income now. Education and health conditionalities may affect children's later outcomes; neither current national Gini series measures intergenerational mobility. Keep this as a mechanism and limit, not as evidence of a future national Gini decline.

"Transfers versus labor income" is an accounting distinction for explaining *contemporaneous national income inequality*, not a claim that CCTs have no immediate effect or that the channels are causally independent. A targeted payment can raise recipient income as soon as it is received, while schooling and health may affect the same children's earnings many years later. A national Gini trend alone cannot identify either program's effect. An income-source decomposition estimates contributions to the observed change but is not by itself a causal impact evaluation.
