# Brazil–Mexico inequality comparison window

## Reassessment: 2008–2014 is not a fully comparable main window

The earlier recommendation to use **2008–2014 as a fully comparable main window was incorrect**. Mexico's ENIGH series is treated as one segment for 2008–2014, but Brazil's World Bank poverty and inequality series crosses a PNAD → PNAD Contínua change in 2012. The World Bank explicitly says these Brazilian surveys are not strictly comparable. Thus the 2008–2014 Brazil–Mexico Gini and bottom-20 changes may still be shown as raw WDI observations, but not as clean within-country changes or a clean comparison of changes.

The World Bank's *Mexico Poverty and Equity Assessment* identifies ENIGH breaks between 2006–2008 and 2014–2016 and therefore analyzes 2008–2014 as one Mexican period. This does **not** establish comparability for Brazil. Ending in 2014 separates most of Brazil's 2015–2016 recession from the main period, but that substantive choice cannot fix the Brazilian survey break.

| WDI measure | Brazil 2008 → 2014 | Mexico 2008 → 2014 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Gini index (0–100) | 54.0 → 52.1 (−1.9 points) | 50.8 → 48.9 (−1.9 points) |
| Bottom-20 income share | 3.2% → 3.6% (+0.4 pp; +12.5% relative) | 3.9% → 4.7% (+0.8 pp; +20.5% relative) |

The similar Gini decline and different bottom-20 gains are a provisional descriptive puzzle, sensitive to Brazil's survey transition. They do not by themselves explain the role of CCTs versus labour income, other transfers, or changes elsewhere in the distribution.

**Potential cleaner designs to test:** (1) retain 2008–2014 but recalculate both countries with harmonized microdata that uses Brazil's original annual PNAD in both 2008 and 2014 and Mexico's ENIGH in both years; IBGE confirms the original PNAD continued through 2015, but its published Gini is not automatically the same construct as WDI's; (2) use 2012–2014 (both countries' post-break survey segments, but only two years); (3) use 2016–2018 (both post-break survey segments, but a different policy/economic period); or (4) use approximate early-2000s comparisons from a harmonized SEDLAC study using country-specific survey years. Before selecting any window, verify the exact survey series, income definition, and comparability flags for *both countries and both indicators*. Mexico's 2014–2016 break makes a 2014-to-2016 change unsuitable even though Brazil's PNADC is continuous there.

## Context and sensitivity

- Show the longer series in the background, **marking** the Mexican 2006–2008 and 2014–2016 breaks and Brazil's 2011–2012 PNAD/PNADC transition. Do not compare endpoints across these breaks as if the measures were fully consistent.
- The earlier 2004–2014 and 2008–2014 calculations may be reported as raw descriptive arithmetic with explicit break warnings, but should not be the primary estimate of a continuous trend.
- The annotated bottom-20 figure also reports a longer descriptive change using common observed years, 1998–2024: Brazil 2.4% to 3.9% (+1.5 percentage points; +62.5% relative; 1.63× the initial share); Mexico 3.3% to 5.6% (+2.3 percentage points; +69.7% relative; 1.70× the initial share). This longer Mexico change crosses both the 2006–2008 and 2014–2016 survey breaks, so it should not be treated as an equally comparable continuous-period estimate.
- Treat Brazil's 2014–2019 deterioration as a separate recession/recovery episode, rather than extending the main CCT comparison through the Mexican 2014–2016 break.
- The bottom-20 figure annotates Brazil's 2014–2019 decline (3.6% to 3.1%) with the 2014–2016 recession and slow recovery. The [World Bank's 2020 Brazil note](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/375591598346747067/pdf/Brazil-s-Poverty-and-Inequality-Since-the-2014-2016-Domestic-Crisis.pdf) describes job losses and a recovery that had not reached the poorest by 2019. The annotation is context, not an attribution of the exact WDI share change.
- The bracket is explicitly labeled **Brazil**. Mexico experienced slower growth and stagnation by 2019, but not the same 2015–2016 recession: [IMF figures](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2019/11/05/pr19397-mexico-imf-executive-board-concludes-2019-article-iv-consultation) show positive Mexican GDP growth in 2015–2018. Mexico's bottom-20 series also crosses the 2014–2016 ENIGH method break, so its apparent rise over that interval should not be interpreted as a clean response to the Brazilian crisis.
- Brazil's 2020 spike (3.1% in 2019 to 4.5% in 2020, then 3.3% in 2021) is annotated with pandemic emergency cash aid (Auxílio Emergencial). [IBGE](https://agenciadenoticias.ibge.gov.br/en/agencia-news/2184-news-agency/news/32292-proportion-of-households-receiving-money-from-other-social-programs-in-2020-rose-to-23-7-driven-by-emergency-aid) and the [World Bank's Brazil Poverty and Equity Assessment](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099230007062256153/pdf/P1746910e33a8407d0b0850b8f0f5bcf18c.pdf) identify emergency transfers as a major reason for lower measured inequality in 2020. This does not imply that pandemic hardship disappeared.
- The school-attendance chart uses UIS household-survey years 2004, 2006, 2008, 2012, and 2014. Its 2004–2014 change also spans the Mexican survey change; the 2008–2014 observations are a more cautious companion to the inequality comparison.
