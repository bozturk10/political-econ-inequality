# Brazil and Mexico: theory, hypothesis, evidence

**Speaking time:** about 2–3 minutes (approximately 315 words). **Common comparison window:** 2003–2014 for both countries. Progresa began before this window; it continued as Oportunidades during the period.

## Draft script

Our puzzle is why inequality appears to have fallen more in Brazil than in Mexico, even though both used conditional cash transfers. I use Evelyne Huber and John D. Stephens’s 2012 argument to develop one possible explanation. They argue that democracy gives left parties an opportunity to build more progressive social policy. The premise is that inequality reflects political decisions about wages and social spending, as well as the operation of markets. Their account also recognizes other influences, including the international economy. [1]

My hypothesis is that Bolsa Família worked alongside other Brazilian policies. A rising minimum wage could compress low-end earnings before transfers, while cash transfers and benefits linked to the minimum wage could raise income after transfers. In Mexico, Progresa and Oportunidades could reduce poverty, but a relatively static real minimum wage would provide less help through the wage channel. This is a proposed mechanism, rather than a claim that the transfer itself caused the difference.

The clearest comparable policy series is the ILO’s real national minimum wage index, with 2000 set to 100 within each country. Brazil rose from 117.4 in 2003 to 203.6 in 2014, a **73% real increase**. Mexico moved from 101.2 to 101.7 over the **same 2003–2014 period**, an increase of about **0.5%**. [2, 3] These indices show changes in purchasing power within each country, not which country had the higher wage level.

There is evidence that labour income mattered in Brazil. A World Bank decomposition of Brazil’s 2004–2013 Gini decline attributes about **41% to labour income** and **39% to non-labour income**, a category broader than Bolsa Família. The same report documents a falling skill premium, while also identifying economic demand as a possible driver. [4] This fits the theory’s emphasis on policy beyond CCTs, but it does not isolate the effect of left government or the minimum wage.

One qualification is essential: our existing World Bank Gini observations for 2008 and 2014 fall by **1.9 points in each country**. Brazil’s survey methodology also changes in this interval. So I would present the stronger Brazil-versus-Mexico decline as a question to test with harmonized **market and disposable income** series, not as an established result of these particular figures. [5, 6]

**What remains to test:** A harmonized annual or consistent survey-year decomposition of market versus disposable income for both countries over the chosen windows. The real minimum wage series supports one proposed channel; it is not a measure of actual wages earned by low-skilled workers. A Brazil–Mexico skill-premium comparison should be added only after extracting identically defined SEDLAC estimates for both countries and checking survey years.
