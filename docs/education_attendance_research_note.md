# Brazil–Mexico CCT comparison: school attendance evidence

## Indicator and coverage

UNESCO UIS `NART.3.Q1.F`: total net attendance rate for girls of official upper-secondary age in the poorest household-wealth quintile. The numerator includes attendance **at any education level**, not just upper secondary. `NART.3.F` is the corresponding rate for all girls of that age. The poorest quintile is defined within each country, usually using a household asset index.

The common observed years in 2004–2014 are **2004, 2006, 2008, 2012, and 2014**. Brazil has observations in 10 of 11 years (2010 missing); Mexico has observations in the even years only. The chart uses only the five common years. The CSV preserves every country-year, including explicit blanks.

| Country | Poorest girls, 2004 | Poorest girls, 2014 | Change | All-girls minus poorest-girls gap, 2004 | Gap, 2014 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Brazil | 72.8% | 81.4% | +8.6 pp | 9.5 pp | 3.0 pp |
| Mexico | 45.3% | 63.7% | +18.4 pp | 16.4 pp | 10.8 pp |

The between-country difference in poorest-girls attendance fell from 27.5 to 17.7 percentage points. Both countries improved, but Mexico began at a much lower level and had a larger increase. The within-country attendance gap between all girls and poorest-quintile girls narrowed in both countries. These are descriptive comparisons, not estimates of CCT effects.

## Use in the presentation

Possible motivating question: **Why did school attendance among girls in the poorest quintile rise in both Brazil and Mexico between 2004 and 2014, but with different starting levels and rates of change?**

Two explanations to investigate with existing research:

1. **CCT programme design and reach:** eligibility, benefit size, school-attendance requirements, and coverage of poor households may have differed.
2. **Broader education expansion:** school supply, access, demographic changes, and other policies may explain part of the trend independently of CCTs.

Use published impact evaluations for any causal claim about Bolsa Família or Progresa/Oportunidades. National household-survey series alone cannot distinguish programme effects from these other changes. Also check comparability before treating the 2008 Mexico shift from ENIGH to MCS-ENIGH in the UIS source notes as a continuous survey series.
